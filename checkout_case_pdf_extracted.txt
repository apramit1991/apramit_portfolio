My Problem Statement
The business is losing revenue because mobile checkout abandonment
(~70%) and low mobile conversion (~2.9%) significantly underperform
benchmarks.
My Goal Statement


===== PAGE 3 =====

Reduce mobile cart abandonment by optimizing the checkout experience.
Secondary goals included increasing mobile checkout completion rate,
lowering payment error recovery drop off, and improving accessibility
compliance to WCAG AA across all checkout components.
The Scope
To solve this problem, the scope had to be narrowed to the checkout flow.
Traffic acquisition and product discovery was working but even though users
were successfully reaching product pages, revenue was being lost at
checkout.
The project focuses on optimizing the core mobile purchase funnel: Product
page →  Cart →  One-page mobile checkout.
Competitive Analysis Using Amazon, Shopify, and Stripe
Key takeaways:
Never force account creation. Place Create Account after payment
confirmation on the success screen.
Do not show all fields at once. Use a progressive disclosure pattern such
as Information then Shipping then Payment to make the page feel
shorter.
Place a lock icon or Secure Checkout text directly under the Pay Now
button. This microcopy improves conversion by addressing trust anxiety.
Secondary Research and Comparative Analysis

===== PAGE 4 =====

According to secondary research from Baymard Institute cart abandonment
statistics for 2025 and comparative analysis, the three leading causes of
checkout abandonment are:
1. Lack of trust in providing credit card information at 19 percent.
2. Forced account creation at 19 percent
3. Checkout being too long or complex at 18 percent.
Image of stat from Reasons for Abandoning Online Purchases at Checkout. Credit: Baymard


===== PAGE 5 =====

How I addressed each abandonment driver in my case study.
1. Lack of trust in providing credit card information at 19 percent
I used trust signals and secure payment elements to increase perceived and
actual security.
This aligns with PCI Security Standards Council requirements for data
integrity and confidentiality, including the use of tokenized payment fields
such as Stripe Elements and visible security indicators near the payment
form.

According to Baymard Institute research in 2023, users respond differently
to different types of security indicators.
SSL seals are issued by certificate authorities such as Norton, Thawte,
and Trustwave. These indicate that the communication between the
browser and the site is encrypted using SSL or TLS.
Trust seals typically indicate third party verification of the business or
website, such as Google or TRUSTe.
Baymard has found that most users respond more strongly to recognizable trust
brands even though SSL technically provides the real security. Perception of safety
is driven more by the logo than by the protocol behind the scenes.

I therefore used a neutral padlock icon with Secure Checkout microcopy.
While it is not a third party brand, it communicates safety at the exact
moment users hesitate, which is directly under the payment action.

2. Forced account creation at 19 percent
Users resist creating accounts during checkout because they do not perceive
immediate value. It requires sharing personal information, remembering
new credentials, and committing to a relationship they did not ask for.
Instead of forcing registration, I allowed guest checkout and positioned
account creation as a benefit. The microcopy explained value such as faster
reorders, order tracking, and access to offers.

===== PAGE 9 =====

A screenshot of the sign in option along with a caption of benefits of signing in from case study.
When users chose to create an account, you reduce friction by collecting
only name and password initially. Additional data such as saved payment
methods was deferred until after trust was established


===== PAGE 10 =====

Image of simple sign up option and guest checkout from Nike. Credit: ConvertCart
3. Checkout being too long or complex at 18 percent
Long forms increase cognitive load and error rates. I used progressive
disclosure with collapsible sections for Information, Address, and Payment
so the checkout felt shorter even when the total fields remained the same.
I removed nonessential fields and implemented real time validation to
prevent submission errors that would force users to re enter data.

I also supported browser autofill and third party wallets. This not only
reduced friction but also addressed another major abandonment driver
which is limited payment options.

Key Takeaways
Mobile conversion problems are checkout problems, not traffic problems.
Trust is a visual and emotional signal, not just a technical one.
Guest checkout consistently outperforms forced registration.
Progressive disclosure increases completion without reducing data
quality.
Microcopy placed at the point of hesitation directly impacts revenue.
Conclusion
Mobile conversion is rarely fixed by more marketing. It is fixed by removing
the small moments of doubt that compound into abandonment.
In this case study, the biggest gains came from three changes that required
no new traffic, no discounts, and no feature bloat. We made the checkout
feel safer, shorter, and more respectful of the user’s time.


===== PAGE 13 =====

When seventy percent of your users arrive on a phone, every unnecessary
field, every forced login, and every missing trust signal quietly taxes
revenue. Optimizing mobile checkout should be your growth strategy.
If you are losing money on mobile, the answer is almost never at the top of
the funnel. It’s waiting at the Pay Now button.
